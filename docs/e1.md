**Status, 27 November 2026: Step 0 is measured and read (Section 10); Step 1 is registered and confirmed by the
author, with B1 behind his sign-off (Section 11).** The direction and four decisions are the author's, and so are

E1 is the first experiment after E0 was closed with a negative result (`docs/e0.md`, Section 10: the Block-DFM

It leaves the preprint's protocol (Sec. 6.2 and after), by the author's decision of 25 November 2026: a change of

How numbers are labelled here: *measured* (by which run, on which device, runtime and precision), *reported* (by
which source), *computed* (arithmetic, no model involved) or *predicted*. A cell printed as `--` is filled by
this protocol.

## 1. The question, and what is already decided

**The question.** On the released `google/gemma-4-E2B-it`, frozen: can a drafter whose tokens the model itself
verifies produce *the model's own text* faster than (a) the model decoding alone and (b) the drafter Google
ships for this model, on the machines and in the runtime of a local product?

Under exact verification the output is the frozen model's, so there is no quality to trade: the results of E1 are
**the exact-match rate, the accepted tokens per cycle and the wall-clock speed**, each named with its device,
runtime and precision. Benchmark scores (IFEval), repetition and perplexity are checks of exactness and
workloads, not results (decision 1).

**What changed the design.** Google released Multi-Token Prediction drafters for the Gemma 4 family on
5 May 2026 under Apache 2.0 (`google/gemma-4-E2B-it-assistant` for this model). All of the following is
*reported* (Gemma 4 Technical Report, arXiv:2607.02770, Sec. 2.6; "Efficient and Robust Drafting with Multi-Token
Prediction", arXiv:2611.25813; LiteRT-LM's Gemma 4 page; the sources are listed in the project note
`claude/e1-lossless-drafting-notes.md`), none of it measured by us:

| | |
|---|---|
| The drafter | one 4-layer head (3 local layers, 1 global), width 256 and 76M parameters for E2B, applied autoregressively; its input is the target's last-layer activation with the token embedding; it attends to the target's KV cache and keeps none of its own; a clustered output layer (2,048 clusters of 128 tokens, exact logits for the top 32 clusters) |
| Speed, E2B | 1.97x in vLLM on an H100 (bf16, batch 1: 278.8 to 550.1 tok/s; block efficiency 2.71 at draft length 4); in LiteRT-LM up to 2.2x on mobile GPUs and up to 1.5x on mobile CPUs, and for E2B on a CPU "a possible slight slowdown" on free-form text |
| An independent measurement (github.com/iprajax/gemma4-mtp, MacBook M4 Pro) | 1.71x in LiteRT-LM on the GPU (84.2 to 144.3 tok/s) but 1.04x in Hugging Face Transformers (21.9 to 22.9 tok/s); outputs diverged at longer lengths in bf16 |
| Adaptation | fine-tuning the target alone lowers the drafter's block efficiency by 21 to 36% (2.51 to 1.60 and 3.73 to 2.95 on two tasks); distilling the drafter again restores most of it |

So the author's Option A (a top-layer drafter with exact verification) exists first-party for this backbone, and
its reported gain depends on the runtime. E1 is therefore defined against it, and starts by measuring it here.

**Decided by the author on 25 November 2026** (Section 7 has his words):

1. E1 is defined against Google's drafter and starts with Step 0: baselines without training, the ceiling, and
   the E0 bridge. The contributions it aims at are the ceiling analysis for local machines, one-shot dependent
   block drafting (arm A3), adaptation to Korean and public-sector text, and the bridge from E0 to verified
   decoding.
2. The downloads: `google/gemma-4-E2B-it`, Google's drafter, the IFEval checker, UltraChat-200K (for research and
   prototyping; its licence is audited before any commercial distribution).
3. Further runtimes on the server, LiteRT-LM and vLLM, in environments of their own.
4. "Local PC" means enterprise and public-sector laptops with an integrated GPU (Intel Core Ultra, AMD Ryzen AI,
   Apple M-series). On the server, Step 0 measures the L4 (a discrete-GPU upper bound, fast to iterate on) and
   the CPU with a fixed number of threads (a CPU-only lower bound). Before any product claim the same LiteRT-LM
   measurement runs on a physical laptop of that kind.

## 2. Before any measurement: setup and preflight (`scripts/ncp/e1_setup.sh`)

One command runs three steps in tmux; each can be run again and skips what is complete.

| `runtimes` | creates three environments: vLLM, LiteRT-LM, and the IFEval checker's packages | `runs/assets/venvs/` |

The server's rules are relaxed here and only here, by decision 3: the script uses the network; it creates
environments besides text-VPA's `.venv`, inside `runs/`, so that no other torch reaches the training environment;
it adds `pyarrow` to text-VPA's `.venv` if that is missing (it reads the parquet files; it pins nothing). Foreign
runtimes run with their home directory under `runs/assets/home`, so that what they cache stays inside `runs/`.
Nothing is written outside `runs/`, and nothing shuts the server down.

A line of the preflight that starts with `UNEXPECTED` is a failed expectation; Step 0 does not start before each
is understood. The smoke decode is a check that the pieces fit, not a result.

**The IFEval checker is not repeatable as shipped** (*measured* in the development sandbox, google-research
`20fadc5`, on the GPT-4 responses the repository ships). Its language checks use `langdetect`, which is
randomised, and `instructions.py` draws from Python's `random` module in 24 places. With only
`langdetect.DetectorFactory.seed = 0` set, three runs on the same responses gave strict prompt-level accuracy
0.7722, 0.7704, 0.7704 and loose 0.7981, 0.7944, 0.7981: a prompt or two of 540 changed sides. With
`random.seed(0)` set as well, three runs in one environment and two in a second gave the same four numbers:
strict 0.7722 / 0.8377, loose 0.7981 / 0.8570 (prompt-level / instruction-level, on the 540 of 541 prompts that
the shipped responses cover; the paper, arXiv:2311.07911, reports 76.89 / 83.57 and 79.30 / 85.37 for GPT-4).
The preflight repeats this self-test on the server. Consequence: C9 below.

## 3. Step 0: what is measured (nothing is trained)

The target is always the released checkpoint, frozen. A response is one user turn in the model's own chat
template with the generation prompt, no system prompt, thinking off. Decoding is greedy in every arm.

**Development prompts** (`chat_vpa/e1_prompts.py`): the first 200 first user turns of UltraChat-200K's
`test_sft` split that have at least one non-blank character and at most 4,000 characters, in file order; nothing
is drawn at random. `train_sft`, from which Step 1 would take its training prompts, is disjoint from it.
Everything that is tuned in E1 is tuned on this file. IFEval is not read in Step 0.

### 3.1 Baselines in Hugging Face Transformers (`chat_vpa/e1_decode.py`)

All arms go through the released model's own `generate`, batch 1, `do_sample=False`:

| Arm | What decodes |
|---|---|
| `ar` | the model alone, one pass per token (the baseline) |
| `mtp:G`, G in {2, 4, 6, 8} | with Google's drafter (`assistant_model=`): G drafted tokens per cycle, one target pass over G + 1 positions keeps the drafted tokens that equal its own argmax and adds its next token |

### 3.2 The ceiling (`chat_vpa/e1_ceiling.py`)

Speculative decoding replaces n target passes over one token by one pass over n tokens; it can only pay where
that pass is cheaper than the n it replaces. On the target alone, for the first 20 prompts (5 on the CPU): the
prompt is prefilled, 32 tokens are decoded one pass at a time, and one pass over the first n of them is timed
from the same prompt cache, for n in {1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 8, 12, 16, 24, 32}.

    t(n)         median seconds of a pass over n new positions (measured)
    ceiling(n)   = n t(1) / t(n): the speed-up of a drafter that costs nothing and is always right (predicted)
    P(n, alpha)  = E t(1) / t(n), E = (1 - alpha^n) / (1 - alpha): the speed-up of a free drafter whose tokens
                 are accepted independently with probability alpha, alpha in {0.5, ..., 0.9} (predicted)

The same passes answer the numerical question behind "lossless": at how many positions does a pass over n tokens
choose another token than n passes over one token (`argmax differs`)? In exact arithmetic, none. Runs: L4 in
bfloat16, L4 in float32 if it fits, CPU in float32 with 4 threads if the memory is free (as in 3.1).

t(1) is a bare pass of the model; `generate` adds its own work per token to every arm of 3.1, which the profiled
run shows.

### 3.3 The E0 bridge (`chat_vpa/e1_bridge.py`)

Orthrus (arXiv:2605.12825) and Uno (arXiv:2609.04010) use E0's construction, a trainable diffusion view that
reads the frozen model's KV cache, as a *drafter* that the frozen model verifies. The bridge measures what E0's
two checkpoints (`runs/e0-T16`, `runs/e0-R16`) are worth in that role as they are, on E0's own evaluation prompts
(MBPP validation, 90 problems, the base checkpoint they were trained on):

1. y is the frozen backbone's greedy continuation by its own `generate` (up to `<eos>`, the truncation rule's
   stop, or 256 tokens).
2. Along y: at position s the drafter drafts B = 16 tokens after (prompt, y[:s]) with K passes of the block
   sampler at temperature 0; a is the number of leading draft tokens equal to y; the cycle yields a + 1 tokens
   (capped at the end of y) and costs K draft passes and 1 verifying pass:

       tokens per backbone pass = tokens / (cycles (K + 1));        autoregressive decoding = 1.

   With nothing ever accepted this is 1 / (K + 1): 0.5, 0.33 and 0.2 at K = 1, 2, 4 (*computed*).
3. The same drafts at training's block boundaries (s = 0, 16, 32, ...): accepted tokens per block, and the
   agreement with y per slot.

K in {1, 2, 4}, prefix-growing and confidence orders. K = 1 uses a step that E0 did not train. Passes are
counted, not timed. A check first runs the committed stream itself as the drafter on 10 problems (every draft
must be accepted in exact arithmetic; a rejection is a numerical difference between `generate` and this
repository's path in bfloat16, the precision E0 ran in).

### 3.4 Other runtimes

A speed-up measured in one runtime is not a speed-up in another: the cost of a pass is a different mix of
arithmetic and overhead in each. Each runtime is its own table.

**LiteRT-LM** (`chat_vpa/e1_litert.py`; built). Google's on-device runtime with its own build of the model (its
quantisation; the drafter is inside the file): the runtime the product would ship in, and the one in which the
drafter's gain is reported. Modes `off`, `on` and `adaptive` (the runtime speculates only while it measures that
to be faster), lenience 0 (the lossless setting) passed explicitly, the sampler at top-k 1, thinking off, the
first 50 development prompts x 256 tokens, the modes back to back per prompt. Reported: decode tok/s by the
runtime's own timer, end-to-end tok/s by the wall clock, the speed-up over `off` with a paired bootstrap, and how
many responses have the same text as `off`'s. Backends: the CPU with 4 threads, and the GPU if the runtime can
use it (on Linux its GPU backend needs a Vulkan driver; if the L4 offers none, the GPU row is reported as not
run). The texts need not equal 3.1's (another build of the weights): exactness is read inside one runtime.
For the GPU backend the Vulkan loader is shown NVIDIA's driver only (`VK_DRIVER_FILES`), so that the row cannot
run on a software renderer; the summary records the driver file.

**vLLM** (`chat_vpa/e1_vllm.py`; written on 25 November 2026, after the preflight had shown vLLM 0.34.0 and its
option, at the author's request, and before any vLLM number exists). The runtime of Google's reported 1.97x for
E2B (on an H100); a server engine, not a local one, so on the L4 it bounds what the discrete GPU can do and is not
the product's number. vLLM 0.34.0 maps Google's assistant checkpoint (`gemma4_assistant`) to its Gemma 4 MTP
drafter, which reads the target's KV cache, when the speculative configuration says `"method": "mtp"` (its source,
`vllm/config/speculative.py`, and its documentation of Gemma 4 assistants, which names E2B as supported).

- *Arms*: `off` (the model alone) and `mtp:G` for G in {2, 4, 6, 8} (C5), with `{"method": "mtp", "model": <the
  assistant checkpoint>, "num_speculative_tokens": G}`; the first 100 development prompts x 256 new tokens (C3,
  C4); greedy (C2: temperature 0, vLLM's argmax path); one request at a time.
- *Engine settings*, the same for every arm: bfloat16, seed 0, a context of 4,096 tokens, prefix caching off (a
  prompt of the warm-up, or a prefix seen before, is prefilled again), the multimodal encoders off
  (`language_model_only`), request statistics on (the engine's timestamps of each request), vLLM's per-request
  acceptance counts for the speculative arms; CUDA graphs and compilation as vLLM's defaults; the prompt is the
  token ids of `e1_common.chat_ids`, as in 3.1.
- *One engine per arm, and one process per engine*: vLLM holds the GPU for an engine's life, so the arms cannot
  alternate prompt by prompt as 3.5 asks. Each arm decodes every prompt after 2 warm-up prompts, and the
  reference runs first and again last (`off-1` and `off-2`, in the order off, mtp:2, mtp:4, mtp:6, mtp:8, off).
  Speed-ups are against `off-1`; `off-2` against `off-1` is the drift of the machine over the run.
- *Reported*: decode tok/s (tokens after the first over the engine's time from the first token to the last),
  end-to-end tok/s (over the wall-clock time of the generate call), tokens per cycle and the share of draft tokens
  accepted (vLLM's counts), the prefill time, the speed-ups with paired-bootstrap intervals, the engine's start-up
  time, and whether each response has the same tokens as `off-1`'s.
- *Reading*: R1 and R2 as for every table, with `off-1` as the reference. R2 is not read for this table if the
  drift's decode interval excludes 1 (the machine's speed moved during the run); the table is then reported as it
  is. A smoke decode of one prompt with `mtp:4` in the preflight checks first that the drafter loads and drafts.

### 3.5 How time is measured, in every runtime

- Batch 1. One GPU job at a time; the summary names the device, runtime, precision and thread count.
- The first prompts are decoded and thrown away (2; 1 on the CPU), by every arm.
- The arms of a prompt run back to back, so that a drift of the machine falls on all of them.
- The device is synchronised before a clock is read (PyTorch); LiteRT-LM's decode rate is its own record.
- Decode speed leaves out the time to the first token; end-to-end speed includes prompt processing. Both are
  ratios of sums over prompts, tokens over seconds; a speed-up is the ratio of two such rates.
- Intervals: 95% percentile, paired bootstrap over prompts, 10,000 resamples, seed 0 (`e0_report.paired_bootstrap`).
- The same prompts, token budget and stopping rule for every arm of a table.
- A profiled run is never used for a speed-up.

### 3.6 Cost

*Predicted* from the token counts and a guess of the decode rates, not measured: the bridge takes minutes; the
GPU decode run about 1.5 to 2 hours (100 prompts x 256 tokens x 6 arms at roughly 25 tok/s); the CPU runs and
LiteRT-LM about an hour together; vLLM under an hour (six engines, a few minutes each to start, then 100 prompts
of up to 256 tokens). `scripts/ncp/e1_step0.sh` runs the parts one after another in tmux, skips what
has finished, and goes on when a run that may not be possible on the machine fails (float32 on the L4 or on the
CPU, LiteRT-LM's GPU backend).

## 4. Reading rules of Step 0 (confirmed by the author, 25 November 2026)

"Clearly" means, as in E0, that the 95% paired-bootstrap interval excludes the value compared with.

**R1, exactness.** For each table (runtime, device, precision): an arm is *exact on this sample* if every one of
its responses equals the reference decoding of the same table (`ar`, or `off`) token for token (LiteRT-LM:
character for character); otherwise it is *not token-identical in this precision*, with the count, the first
differing positions and, for PyTorch, the ceiling's `argmax differs` counts in the same precision beside it. No
speed-up is called lossless from a configuration that is not exact on its sample, and "lossless" is always said
with the runtime and precision.

**R2, whether Google's drafter pays.** For each table, the drafter's best setting is the draft length (or mode)
with the largest decode speed-up; all settings are reported. It *pays clearly* if its interval lies above 1,
*costs clearly* if it lies below 1, and is *undecided* otherwise.

**R3, whether any drafter could pay on a device** (PyTorch, where the ceiling is measured). If ceiling(n) is
below 1.2 for every n up to 8 on a device, speculative decoding cannot gain 20% there whatever the drafter: no
drafter is trained or evaluated for speed on that device in that runtime, and the ceiling curve is the finding.

**R4, which lever is short** (PyTorch, at the drafter's best draft length G, in the precision of the main table).
With tau the measured tokens per cycle:

    acceptance factor  a = tau / (G + 1)                    the share of a cycle's positions that is kept
    cost factor        c = speed-up / (tau t(1) / t(G + 1))  the share of a free drafter's gain that survives
                                                             the drafter's own time and the runtime's overhead

Both are reported with the profiled shares. The smaller factor names the first trained arm of Step 1: the cost
factor points to A3 (fewer drafter calls per cycle), the acceptance factor to A2 (a drafter distilled on the
target's own text). This fixes the order before the numbers are seen; it does not register Step 1.

**R5, the bridge.** A row (checkpoint, K, order) *breaks even* if its tokens per backbone pass are at least 1
(clearly: the interval's lower end is at least 1). Arm B1 (Section 6) enters Step 1 only if some row of T16 breaks
even; otherwise it is deferred behind the A arms and the bridge's table is reported as it is. Whatever the
outcome, the bridge is not a statement about dialogue: it is code, on the base checkpoint.

### 6.1 A3, the block-latent drafter (the author's direction of 25 November 2026)

VPA's pattern carried to drafting: a predictor decides one latent for the next block from what the target has
already computed, and a realiser emits the block's B tokens in one parallel call; the frozen target verifies them.
Google's drafter calls its head once per drafted token, each call fed the token drafted before it. A parallel head
without a latent drafts every position from the verified prefix alone, so where the drafter is unsure between two
continuations its per-position argmaxes can splice them into a block that is neither (Lemma 1 of MFD, Section
6.2, counts these splices). A3 decides the latent first and conditions every position on that one decision.

Why a latent can help under greedy decoding at all: the target's greedy continuation is one sequence, so the
target's own factorisation term is zero (the preprint's Cor. 1). The uncertainty that splits a one-pass draft is
the drafter's, about which continuation the target will produce. A decided latent cannot add information that the
drafter's inputs lack; it can only keep all positions of one draft on one of the drafter's hypotheses. MFD reports
that its mixture gain persists at temperature 0 (Section 6.2).

**Design.** D1 to D8 are open choices, for the author to confirm when Step 1 is registered.

| | Choice | Proposed | Alternatives |
|---|---|---|---|
| D1 | What the latent encodes | the frozen target's own top-layer states over the next B positions of its greedy continuation, pooled by a small encoder that exists only in training: a representation of the future block (a JEPA target), not its tokens | a mixture learned end to end without an encoder (DBLAST); modes assigned by best fit to the realised block (MFD); the target's future hidden states themselves, flow-matched from its recent trajectory and read out by its frozen output layer (ChainFlow) |
| D2 | Discrete or continuous | discrete: a codebook of C codes (vector quantisation). The predictor is a classifier whose argmax is the decision, the code of the true block gives an oracle, and a code carries at most ln C nats per block (the bound of Text-VPA's Prop. 1(a) for a skeleton mode) | continuous and regressed (the prediction is a conditional mean, which lies between modes where the drafter is unsure); continuous and flow-matched in one or two steps (VPA's realiser, as ChainFlow does) |
| D3 | C | 256 (at most 5.5 nats per block, *computed*) | 64; 1,024 (6.9 nats) |
| D4 | The realiser | Google's drafter's architecture, initialised from its weights where shapes match, called once over B query slots. Each slot's input is the target's top-layer activation at the last verified position, with the code's vector and a learned depth embedding in place of a token embedding; each query sits at its own position; the slots attend to the target's KV cache as the drafter's layers do, and not to each other, so the code is their only coordination | attention among the slots of a block (Amphista, DFlash): the drafter's layers take their keys and values from the target and have no projections of their own, so this needs new parameters, a second change |
| D5 | The predictor | one drafter-style layer on the same activation and a linear map to C logits (about a quarter of a head call) | an MLP on the activation |
| D6 | B | A1's best draft length G* in Step 0 (the same verifying pass as A1's best), and 8 | |
| D7 | Decoding | the argmax code, one chain, through the same `generate` call as every other arm (A3 as a candidate generator of it) | the top m codes as m branches of a tree verified in one target pass, MFD's main gain: a verification loop of our own, a later arm |
| D8 | Training | A2's rollouts; per slot, cross-entropy on the greedy token; the vector-quantisation and commitment losses; the predictor's cross-entropy on the encoder's codes. One target pass per rollout, without gradients, gives every block's inputs and its true states, and all blocks of a rollout train in one pass of the head under a staircase mask (E0's training pass, Prop. 1(b)) | an acceptance-oriented objective (DBLAST; arXiv:2610.10411): a second change |

The parameters used in inference stay within A2's plus 5%; the encoder is used only in training.

**Three runs, in this order, each one change from the one before:**

1. **A3-0**, the realiser without a latent (a learned constant vector in the code's place): the one-pass draft
   without guidance, and the control for every claim about the latent.
2. **A3-oracle**, the realiser given the code of the *true* next block (the encoder applied to the target's own
   states of that block). It conditions on the reference: labelled as such and never reported as the method
   (agreement 5). It bounds what a code of this size can do for a one-pass draft.
3. **A3**, the same realiser given the predictor's code: the method. The realiser and the codebook are
   A3-oracle's, so this run trains the predictor only.

**Gates** (rules before runs):

- *Before any training of A3, break-even* (*predicted* from Step 0's measurements, per device and runtime). Let
  T_1 be A1's measured seconds per cycle at its best draft length G*, D_1 the part of it spent in the drafter (the
  profiled run), tau_1 its tokens per cycle, D_3 the drafting seconds of one A3 cycle (estimated as 1.25 calls of
  Google's head until A3 exists, then measured), and t(n) the ceiling's pass times. A3 with blocks of B is faster
  than A1 if and only if

      tau_3 > tau* = tau_1 (T_1 - D_1 + D_3 + t(B + 1) - t(G* + 1)) / T_1.

  At B = G*, A3 may accept fewer tokens per cycle than A1 by the share of the cycle it saves. For example
  (hypothetical numbers, not a prediction): if the drafter takes 30% of A1's cycle at G* = 4, D_3 is about 9% of
  it and tau* is about 0.79 tau_1. Where tau* > B + 1, no drafter of this kind can pay, and A3 is not trained for
  that device and runtime.
- *After A3-0 and A3-oracle.* If A3-oracle's tokens per cycle are not clearly above A3-0's (paired bootstrap over
  the development prompts, counted along the target's greedy continuations as in 3.3), a code of this size cannot
  guide a one-pass draft here: A3 is not trained, and the result is reported as the drafting analogue of the
  predecessor's Stage 1 oracles. If A3-oracle stays below tau*, no predictor can make A3 faster than A1 in that
  table.
- *A3 against A3-0* is the latent's contribution; *A3 against A1 and A2* is the trade-off the author asked for.

**Reported for every arm:** tokens per cycle; the acceptance profile (how often depth k is accepted when every
depth below it is); drafter and verification seconds per cycle (profiled); the decode speed-up over `ar` and over
A1 and A2 in the same table; exactness (R1); and the trade-off plane, drafter seconds per cycle against tokens per
cycle, with the iso-speed lines of the inequality above. Tokens per cycle counted along the target's greedy
continuation are exact under greedy verification and need no timing; the speed-ups come from the decode loop.

**Where A3 can run:** in PyTorch only. LiteRT-LM runs the drafter packed in its model file, so A3's speed-ups are
PyTorch speed-ups until a runtime implements it; the inequality above, filled with LiteRT-LM's costs where they can
be measured, says what such an implementation would have to reach.

**Cost** (*predicted*; a timing run as in E0 measures it before the runs are registered): the rollouts that A2
needs anyway, 100,000 prompts in 6 to 12 hours on the L4 (100,000 / 4.76 rollouts per second is 5.8 hours,
*computed* from the rate of E0's writer, *measured* on 256-token code completions; chat responses run longer);
A3-0 and A3-oracle a fraction of an E0 run each (about 12 hours, *measured*), because the target runs without
gradients; A3 a fraction of that.

### 6.2 Prior work on block latents for one-pass drafting

*Reported* by the sources, read on 25 November 2026 from their summaries and abstracts; none of it is measured by
us. The block-latent idea is an active line of work in 2026, and its mechanism is not new:

| Work | The latent | Drafting and verification | Reported |
|---|---|---|---|
| DBLAST (arXiv:2608.05448, Huawei Canada, August 2026) | a categorical variable with K = 4 branches on the output side of a DFlash drafter, its prior from the block anchor's state; a learned mixture, no encoder | one branch drawn from the prior and decoded in one pass; speculative sampling | acceptance length 5.6% to 12.1% above DFlash on Qwen3-8B (5.8% to 9.3% on Qwen3-4B), the larger gains for the less deterministic targets; latency per iteration 1.4% to 1.6% higher; no wall-clock speed-up |
| Multi-Future Drafting, MFD (arXiv:2611.13017, Beihang University and Canva) | K = 8 mode embeddings added to every position of a block; each training block assigned to its best-fitting mode (winner-take-all), and a gate trained to predict that mode | the K copies of the block in one pass; a tree over the modes' prefixes | at temperature 0 on Qwen3-4B, 6.73x (9.71 tokens per round) against 6.08x (8.59) for DDTree, 4.38x (5.98) for a DFlash chain and 4.21x (6.46) for EAGLE-3; the gain persists at temperature 0 |
| ChainFlow (arXiv:2611.08669) | a VAE latent of the target's final hidden states; one or two flow-matching steps from an extrapolation of the recent latents to the next 8 positions; tokens read out by the frozen output layer | 8 positions in one pass, a beam into an 8 x 4 tree; greedy | on Qwen3.5-4B (vLLM, one B300) 1.39x faster than EAGLE-3 and 1.11x faster than DFlash, 1.87x over plain decoding; 1.81x after adaptation to Turkish; output differed from plain greedy decoding on 4% to 13% of prompts (fp16 near-ties) |
| Parallel Token Prediction (arXiv:2512.21323, UC Irvine) | the sampling noise, as an input | interdependent tokens in one call | 7.0 tokens per step against 6.2 for independent prediction (CodeContests, TinyLlama-1.1B); 4.18 on Spec-Bench (Vicuna-7B) |

Related and not read: CADENCE (arXiv:2610.22788; heads that predict latents of future spans); "Trees from
marginals" (arXiv:2607.06763) and DARTree (arXiv:2608.13524), which MFD names as concurrent ways to make block
drafts dependent; ConFu (arXiv:2603.08899), a drafter that predicts where the generation is heading.

What A3 can still contribute, as far as these sources were read: (1) the comparison the author asked for, a
one-pass latent drafter against the same target's first-party sequential drafter (none of the works above
compares with Google's), on local hardware and in a runtime a product ships in; (2) an oracle bound on the latent
channel (A3-oracle) as a gate before the method is trained; (3) exactness read per runtime and precision
(ChainFlow's 4% to 13% shows why it matters); (4) Korean and public-sector text, where ChainFlow's Turkish
adaptation is the nearest precedent. ChainFlow is VPA's pattern (a few flow steps in the target's latent space,
then the block in one pass), published before us: the paper cannot claim that mechanism as new. Whether a code
supervised by the target's future states (D1) works differently from one assigned by best fit (MFD) or learned as
a mixture (DBLAST) is for A3 to test, not something claimed in advance.

## 9. What Step 0 cannot show

- **Not the target hardware.** The L4 is a discrete GPU and the server's CPU is not a laptop's; decision 4's
  laptop run is a separate measurement. PyTorch in eager mode is not a product runtime.
- **Not the product's text.** English first turns of UltraChat, single turn, at most 256 tokens; no Korean, no
  public-sector documents, no long context.
- **Greedy only** (C2). Nothing is said about sampled decoding.
- **Exactness on a sample.** "Exact on 100 prompts" is a statement about 100 prompts in one precision.
- **No ceiling in LiteRT-LM.** Its Python interface gives no pass over n positions to time, so R3 and R4 are
  read in PyTorch only, and LiteRT-LM contributes R1 and R2.
- **The bridge is not dialogue**, and its drafters were trained for another purpose with about 20M target tokens
  (*computed*: 10,000 steps x 32 examples x about 63 tokens).

and the float32 ceilings never choose another argmax (0 of 2,260 positions on the L4, 0 of 565 on the CPU).
(2) In bfloat16, a pass over 2 to 9 positions, the verifying pass of draft lengths up to 8, chooses another argmax
than one-token passes at 4 of 560 positions (0.71%): if such flips were independent, a response of 230 tokens
(the mean length, 10.3, item 2) would stay identical with probability 0.9929^229 = 0.19 (*computed*); 18 to 26 of
100 are. (3) Prompt lookup, which has no drafter network, is as often non-identical (74 of 100) as Google's
drafter: what changes the argmax is the verifying pass over several positions, not the drafter. (4) The bridge's
committed-stream check, the backbone's own next token drafted through this repository's path in bfloat16, was
accepted at 723 of 727 positions (0.55% rejected), the same order as (2). In vLLM: its plain decoding is
deterministic across engines (`off-2` equals `off-1` on all 100 prompts), and `mtp-2` and `mtp-4` diverge from it
first at the same ten positions, as near-ties that flip between a pass over one position and a pass over several
would; consistent with precision, not shown (no ceiling in vLLM). In LiteRT-LM's GPU backend: not decided (10.4,
flag 1).

### 10.4 Flags: what may be a bug rather than a finding

1. **LiteRT-LM's GPU backend at lenience 0 does not keep the text** (49 of 50 responses differ from `off`, from
   the 4th character on), while its CPU backend, run by the same script with the same settings, keeps it on 50 of
   50. Lenience 0 is documented as the lossless setting. This run cannot tell a precision effect of the GPU path
   (Google's quantised build, the GPU backend's kernels for a pass over several positions) from a defect in the
   runtime, nor whether `off` itself is repeatable on the GPU backend; that `on` and `adaptive` depart from `off`
   at the same ten positions shows the change is deterministic. It is not this repository's script (the CPU rows
   run the same code). Check proposed: Section 11.7, P2.
2. **vLLM's drift rule is stricter than its purpose.** The drift is -0.1% (0.999), but vLLM's timing hardly
   varies from prompt to prompt, so the bootstrap interval is about 0.001 wide and excludes 1. The rule was written
   to catch a kernel compiled during measured requests, which would make `off-2` faster than `off-1`; here `off-2`
   is 0.1% slower, against speed-ups whose intervals start at 1.75 and above. The reading stands as registered (R2
   is not read for this table); a tolerance for Step 1's vLLM tables is proposed (Section 11, S11).
3. **The profiled slice**: `mtp-4` is identical to `ar` on 0 of 10 prompts there, against 26 of 100 in the main
   run, and has 1.94 tokens per cycle against 2.12. At the bfloat16 rate of 10.2, a 230-token response stays
   identical with probability about 0.19, so 0 of 10 has probability about 0.81^10 = 0.12, and the first ten
   prompts are not the hundred's average; not a reason to doubt a reading. Whether the bfloat16 path repeats
   itself between processes can be read without the GPU from the two `samples.jsonl` files (the main run's first
   ten prompts against the profiled run); not done.
4. **Google's drafter accepts fewer tokens here than reported**: 2.18 tokens per cycle at G = 4 in vLLM (2.12 in
   PyTorch in bfloat16, 2.10 in float32), against Google's reported block efficiency of 2.71 at draft length 4 for
   E2B (vLLM on an H100). Two independent implementations of the drafter (Transformers' and vLLM's) agree, so it
   runs as built here; the workload (first turns of UltraChat, thinking off, 256 tokens) is the likely difference,
   and it is not checked.

### 11.4 A2: Google's drafter distilled on the target's rollouts

- **S6, initialisation and what is trained.** A1's weights; every parameter of the drafter is trained (77.7M: its

- **S7, the objective.** At every position of a rollout the drafter is unrolled over G_train = 4 depths as it
  runs at inference: every query at the last verified position, its own hidden state handed from depth to depth,

  change from T16); K in {1, 2}.
- **S14, the budget and the measurement.** E0's 10,000 steps of 32 examples. Measured: tokens per backbone pass
  along the target's greedy continuations of the development prompts (the bridge's count), then the wall-clock in
  PyTorch on the L4 through the one-pass drafting loop that A3 needs too. Alternative: 5,000 steps.

### 11.7 The product track (little GPU time; the author's to schedule)

- **P1, the laptop comes first.** In LiteRT-LM on this server Google's drafter gains at most 5% (Section 10.2, R2,
  CPU and GPU backends), against up to 2.2x on mobile GPUs and up to 1.5x on mobile CPUs that Google reports for
  LiteRT-LM, and 1.71x on an M4 Pro GPU that an independent benchmark reports (*reported*, Section 1). The server
  cannot answer the product question. The LiteRT-LM measurement of Section 3.4 on a physical iGPU or M-series laptop
  (decision 4) should therefore come before any work on putting A2 into LiteRT-LM; `e1_litert.py` runs without
  torch and needs only the model file (2.41 GiB) and the prompt file.
- **P2, LiteRT-LM's GPU exactness on the L4** (approved by the author on 27 November 2026; about ten to fifteen
  minutes of the GPU; `bash scripts/ncp/e1_p2.sh`, `chat_vpa/e1_p2.py`). **Registered on 27 November 2026, before
  the run.** Four runs, each one engine of one mode in a process of its own, on the GPU backend shown only NVIDIA's
  Vulkan driver, with Step 0's settings (lenience 0, top-k 1, the first 10 development prompts x 256 tokens, 2
  warm-up prompts): `off-a`, `on-a`, `off-b`, `on-b`. (The approved outline had `off` twice and `on` once; the
  second `on` run, about two minutes more, also tests whether speculation repeats itself.) Read:
  - P2a, repeatability: `off-a` against `off-b` and `on-a` against `on-b`, the same text on how many of 10 prompts;
    the backend repeats itself if both are 10 of 10.
  - P2b, speculation alone: `on-a` against `off-a` and `on-b` against `off-b`; beside them, each run against
    Step 0's GPU row of its mode (three engines in one process, every Vulkan driver visible).
  - P2c, the parting token: for every pair of P2b and of Step 0's GPU row (`on` and `adaptive` against `off`, 50
    prompts) whose texts differ, both texts are tokenised again by the target's tokenizer, and the released
    checkpoint in float32 scores the prompt (its chat template) with the shared tokens. Recorded: the two parting
    tokens' ranks (1 is the argmax) and log-probabilities. A **near tie**: both tokens are the target's top two and
    their log-probabilities lie within 1 nat; **far**: the speculating side's token ranks below 5 while the plain
    side's ranks among the top 2; **other** otherwise. A pair in which one token sequence ends where the other goes
    on is counted apart and not scored; the same pair (prompt and both texts) is scored once.
  - Verdict: **a precision effect** if at least 90% of the scored pairs are near ties and none is far; **a defect of
    the GPU backend's speculation** if at least 10% are far; **undecided** otherwise. If P2a fails, the verdict adds
    that the backend does not repeat itself, so that what speculation changes is not isolated from what changes
    between two runs of one mode. The float32 checkpoint is not LiteRT-LM's quantised build: its ranks say whether
    the runtime's two choices were close for the model both approximate, not what the runtime computed.
  - Dry run (development sandbox, 27 November 2026): `run` with LiteRT-LM 0.20.0 from PyPI on its 49 MB test model,
    CPU backend: two `off` processes gave the same text; `on` stopped with the runtime's message ("Speculative
    decoding requires embedding lookup"), the test file having no drafter; `read` on the two runs.
  - **Result (27 November 2026, on the L4).** *Undecided* by the registered rule, as the author reported it from
    `bash scripts/ncp/e1_p2.sh show`: the backend repeated itself ("deterministic"), the texts with and without
    speculation were the same on 0 of 10 prompts, and parting tokens lie up to 15.7 nats apart under the float32
    target (*measured* by P2, as reported; the full output is to be pasted for the record). What follows: the
    GPU backend's speculative decoding changes the text on every prompt and repeatably, and whether by precision
    or by a defect stays open; the CPU backend, exact in Step 0, is not concerned, and none of Step 1's PyTorch or
    vLLM tables depends on it.
- **P3, a retrained drafter in LiteRT-LM** (prioritised by the author, 27 November 2026). Read in the sources on
  27 November 2026 (LiteRT-LM at `5731d7c` and litert-torch at `7aef351`, both of 25 November 2026); *reported* by
  those sources, and nothing run on the real model file yet:
  - **The container takes a drafter.** A `.litertlm` file holds it as a TFLite section of its own, of model type
    `tf_lite_mtp_drafter` (`LlmMetadata.TF_LITE_MTP_DRAFTER = 13`; `tf_lite_mtp_aux`, 14, holds auxiliary graphs
    of the NPU executor). The public builder (`litert-lm-builder` on PyPI; `litert-lm pack` and `unpack`) unpacks a
    file into its sections and packs it again, and takes `--model_type mtp_drafter` for that section.
  - **The runtime's contract.** The base model must have a `verify` signature over W positions that also returns its
    `activations`; the runtime drafts W - 1 tokens per round (`num_draft_steps = verifier->width() - 1`), so **the
    draft length is fixed by the file, not chosen at run time** (Step 0 measured the length built into Google's
    file, which is still to be read from it). The drafter is one signature, called once per drafted token: it takes
    the position (`input_pos`), the token's embedding concatenated with the previous activations (1,536 + 1,536 =
    3,072 for E2B), the base model's own cache tensors (`kv_cache_*`, handed over from the base model's buffers) and
    the attention masks, and returns `logits` and `projected_activations`; the runtime samples it greedily and, at
    lenience 0, accepts a draft only where the verifier's own token equals it. This is A1's architecture as
    Transformers runs it, so A2 fits it as long as its shapes stay A1's (S6 keeps them).
  - **No exporter for it.** litert-torch, the documented path from safetensors (`litert-torch export_hf`), cannot
    export the drafter: its `assistant_model` setting (not offered on its command line) only adds the base model's
    `verify` signature (of width `mtp_verifier_step`, 5 by default) and its activations output. Google's drafter
    section was made by tooling that is not public, as far as these sources show.
  - **What follows for the product.** A2 can ship in LiteRT-LM only through a conversion of our own, by one of two
    routes. (a) **Weight substitution**: unpack Google's file, write A2's weights into the drafter section's graph in
    the section's own storage format (quantised as Google's weights are there, to be read from the file), keep the
    base model and every other section unchanged, and pack the file again. (b) **An export of our own**: the drafter
    step written as a module with the runtime's signature and converted by litert-torch's generic converter, with the
    base model's cache tensor names. Either is our tooling to keep working across LiteRT-LM releases, and its first
    test is a null test: Google's own drafter weights, put back by the same tooling, must give the responses of
    Google's file unchanged. Route (a) keeps the base model and the draft length exactly as Google built them and is
    the smaller step: it is the one recommended, once A2 has paid in PyTorch.
  - **Next, without the GPU** (proposed): on the server, unpack the model file with `litert-lm-builder` and record
    the drafter section's size and weight types, the base model's `verify` signatures with their widths, and the
    cache tensor names. `litert-lm-builder` is not in LiteRT-LM's environment; adding it there is the author's to
    approve.
- **The IFEval protocol run** (C9) is registered after A2's development results, with the arms and runtimes they
  select.

### 11.9 What is built (27 November 2026), and what was found while building it

**Built and tested on the CPU** (Section 8): the rollout writer for chat prompts (`e1_rollouts.py`, S2 to S5); A2's
and A3-0's trainer (`e1_train.py`, S6 to S8; D4, D6, D8), which saves `RUN/final` as a `Gemma4AssistantForCausalLM`
directory, so that `generate` and vLLM load A2 as they load A1 (tested: A2 loads, and verified by the target in
float32 it changes no token); the timing runs (`scripts/ncp/e1_step1.sh`); P2's check (27 November 2026). Then,
on 27 November 2026 after the timing runs: A2 beside A1 in one decode run, with the accepted-prefix arms
(`e1_decode.py`'s `--drafter LABEL=PATH`, `D:G` and `D:G:clean`); the readings R1, R2, R6, R7, R9 and S11 over
the decode records (`e1_readings.py`); micro-batches with exact accumulation and the halving on out-of-memory in
the trainer; A2's whole run (`bash scripts/ncp/e1_step1.sh a2`). **Still to build**, each with tests against
independent references: A3's encoder, codebook and predictor (A3-oracle, A3); one drafting loop for one-pass
drafters (A3, and B1 if signed off), inside `generate` as a candidate generator where Transformers allows it,
otherwise a loop of its own tested token for token against `generate` in float32. B1's code waits behind B1's
gate (11.6).

**Choices made while building** (routine, decided without the author; each replaces nothing registered):

1. *The keys the drafter trains with.* A query at position q attends to the target's keys of every position before
   q in the drafter's full-attention layers and of the W + 1 positions before q in its sliding ones (W the
   drafter's configured sliding window): what Transformers 5.21 hands the drafter in the first cycle after a
   prefill (its bidirectional sliding mask is inclusive, distance <= W, over the last entries). A batched training
   call under this rule reproduces Transformers' own single-query call of the drafter in float64 (to 1e-10 with
   SDPA; with eager attention to float32's rounding, since its softmax runs in float32; tested). Not taken: what
   Transformers hands it after a partly accepted cycle (flag 1).
2. *Which positions.* Every q from a response's first token to the one before its last; depth k is scored where
   x_{q+k} exists. A response's stop token is a target (drafting it ends a response a cycle earlier); a response
   ended by the budget has no target after its last token. The targets are the rollouts' tokens, not the argmax of
   the training pass, which recomputes the target in bfloat16 in batches of 16 with right padding while the
   rollouts came from batches of up to 128 with left padding (in bfloat16 the two can differ, Section 10.2).
3. *Equal weights* (S7): each depth's loss is the mean over its scored positions, and the loss is the mean over the
   depths (a deeper depth has slightly fewer positions per response).
4. *Where S8 is silent*: gradients clipped at norm 1 (text-VPA's trainers); cosine decay to zero; the 3% warm-up is
   375 of 12,500 steps; A2 is saved in float32.
5. *A3-0* (D4): the code starts at the target's input embedding of `<mask>` (text-VPA's mask vector started there),
   the depth vectors at zero; every parameter of the realiser is trained, as A2's are (S6); the slots weigh
   equally; A2's per-position loss, the cluster term included (the author's choice on flag 2, 27 November 2026).
   Its budget is A2's (2 epochs, 12,500 steps of 16), which 11.8's cost assumed and the author confirmed on 27
   November 2026; micro-batches of 8.
6. *Held-out curves*: on S2's 2,000 held-out rollouts at step 0, every 2,500 steps and at the end; per depth, how
   often the drafter's choice is the target's token, and the tokens per cycle that choice gives along the
   rollouts' greedy continuations (the first token, which the prefill gives, not counted). Counts, not decode
   measurements; never used for R6 or R7.
7. *Development prompts among the training prompts*: the writer counts the training prompts whose text equals a
   development prompt (exactly, or after collapsing whitespace) and records their ids; nothing is excluded, since
   S2 has no such rule (flag 3).

**Found while building, for the author:**

1. **Transformers hands its drafter the keys of rejected drafts.** Transformers 5.21's
   `SinglePositionMultiTokenCandidateGenerator` cuts the target's keys, which it keeps from the verifying pass, at
   the sequence's length. *Measured* on the tiny model in float32 (CPU; kept as a strict `xfail` test in
   `tests/test_e1_train.py`): after the first cycle, every cycle that accepted n < G drafts handed the drafter at
   q the keys of q + 1 full-attention positions where the accepted prefix has q (the last one is a rejected
   draft's), and the drafter's output differed from its output with the accepted prefix's keys. By the code, the
   sliding window it sees then ends at the last rejected draft (G - n of its W + 1 keys are rejected drafts', and
   the G - n oldest are missing), and only the first cycle after a prefill and cycles that accept every draft are
   clean (the first was clean in the measurement; none there accepted every draft). Exactness is not affected
   (the target verifies every draft); acceptance in PyTorch may be lower than the drafter allows, for A1 and A2
   alike, since every PyTorch arm goes through this call. Step 0's vLLM tokens per cycle were above PyTorch's
   bfloat16 ones at every G (1.91, 2.18, 2.30, 2.31 against 1.88, 2.12, 2.21, 2.24; Section 10.1, *measured*):
   consistent with this, and no evidence of it (another runtime, other numerics, different responses). vLLM's and
   LiteRT-LM's code for this step was not read. *For the author:* (a) keep S9 as registered, every PyTorch arm
   through Transformers' own call; or (b) add one PyTorch arm per drafter, the same candidate generator with the
   keys cut to the accepted prefix, beside the registered arms (a change to S9; "a few minutes of the GPU per
   table" was too low: about 15 minutes per arm in the main table). Recommended: (b), the registered arms
   unchanged. **Decided by the author on 27 November 2026: (b)** (S9 as amended in 11.4, with R9).
2. **A3-0's loss and its head.** A3's realiser is Google's drafter, which drafts the best token among its 32 top
   clusters (of 2,048; Section 1). D8's loss trains the token's logit but not the centroid layer, so
   the clusters in which A3-0 looks can drift from its best token. The held-out curves report both choices. Before
   A3-0's full run, one of: (a) D8 as written, with A3 drafting the best token over the whole vocabulary (8 x
   262,144 x 256 = 537M multiply-adds per cycle for the full output layer, *computed*); or (b) S7's per-position
   loss for A3's slots too (`--set cluster_weight=1`), with A3 drafting as Google's head does. Recommended: (b): by
   11.2, A3-0's one change from A2 is one call over B slots, and a loss without the cluster term would be a second
   change. **Decided by the author on 27 November 2026: (b)**; the trainer's default is now the cluster term at
   weight 1 for both arms.
3. **Development prompts among the training prompts.** The timing run counts them over all 100,000 training prompts
   (`show`). If there are any, the author decides whether they leave the training set (a change to S2). **Measured
   on 27 November 2026: none**; nothing is excluded.
